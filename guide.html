Chapter 1 — The "What to Buy" Framework
Fundamental Analysis
These 10 metrics reveal if the underlying business is genuinely healthy and worth owning
📈
Fundamental
Earnings & Revenue Growth
YoY % Change in Revenue & EPS

Think of revenue as the total money the company collects from customers. Earnings (profit) is what remains after every expense is paid. When both grow consistently year after year, the business is genuinely expanding — not just surviving. This is the single most important signal that a stock can go higher.

📖 Deep Dive + What to Look For
▼
🟢 Bullish Signals
Revenue growing >10–15% year-over-year consistently
Earnings (EPS) growing faster than revenue — expanding margins
Management consistently beats Wall Street earnings estimates
🔴 Warning Signals
Revenue declining two or more quarters in a row
Earnings growing but revenue shrinking (unsustainable — costs are just being cut)
Real Example
NVIDIA (NVDA) grew revenue from $16B to $61B in just one fiscal year — a 265% jump. The stock tripled. This is the power of explosive earnings growth.
🏷️
Fundamental
Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio
P/E = Stock Price ÷ Earnings Per Share

Imagine a lemonade stand earns $1/year. If someone buys it for $20, they're paying 20× earnings — that's a P/E of 20. The P/E ratio tells you how expensive a stock is relative to the company's actual profits. A high P/E means you're paying a premium; a low P/E could be a bargain — or a warning sign the business is declining.

📖 P/E Benchmarks by Scenario
▼
P/E RangeWhat It SignalsVerdict
< 10Deeply cheap — or business is decliningCaution
10–20Undervalued to fair (non-tech sectors)Good
20–35Fairly priced for solid growth companiesNormal
35–60Priced for high growth — must deliverWatch
> 60Very expensive — must grow into it fastRisky
S&P 500 average P/E is ~25. Tech giants trade at 35–60× because their growth justifies it. Banks and utilities trade at 8–15× due to slow growth. Always compare within the same sector.
🔬
Fundamental
PEG Ratio (Price/Earnings-to-Growth)
PEG = P/E Ratio ÷ Annual EPS Growth Rate (%)

The P/E ratio alone can be misleading. A P/E of 40 looks scary — but if the company is growing earnings at 40%/year, it's actually fairly priced. The PEG ratio fixes this by adding the growth rate into the equation, giving you a smarter, more complete picture of whether the valuation is justified.

📖 Interpretation + Peter Lynch's Rule
▼
Golden Rules
PEG < 1: Undervalued relative to growth — potential bargain
PEG = 1: Fairly priced — paying a fair price for the growth rate
PEG > 2: Overvalued — paying too much for the expected growth
"The P/E ratio of any company that's fairly priced will equal its growth rate."— Peter Lynch, legendary Fidelity Magellan fund manager
⚙️
Fundamental
Return on Equity (ROE)
ROE = Net Profit ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100

ROE measures how efficiently management turns your money into profit. If a company holds $100 of shareholder capital and earns $20 profit, ROE = 20%. High ROE is a sign of an excellent, highly efficient management team that knows how to deploy capital and generate superior returns for investors.

📖 Benchmarks + Real Numbers
▼
Below 10%
Weak
10–20%
Average
Above 20%
Excellent
Apple (AAPL): ROE ~160%. Visa (V): ROE ~45%. These are extraordinary. The S&P 500 average ROE is about 14%.
🏦
Fundamental
Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio
P/B = Stock Price ÷ Book Value Per Share

Book value is what the company would be worth if it liquidated all assets and paid off every debt today. The P/B ratio compares the stock price to this "breakup value." It's especially critical for asset-heavy sectors like banking, insurance, real estate, and manufacturing where physical assets drive the balance sheet.

📖 When P/B Matters + Key Benchmarks
▼
🏦 Most Useful For
Banks, credit unions, and financial institutions
Real estate investment trusts (REITs)
Heavy manufacturing, energy, and industrial companies
📌 Key Numbers
P/B < 1: Trading below asset value — potential deep value buy
P/B 1–3: Normal and acceptable range
P/B > 5: Expensive for asset-heavy businesses
Note: P/B is less meaningful for software and tech companies — their biggest assets (brand, IP, algorithms) don't appear on the balance sheet.
💳
Fundamental
Debt-to-Equity (D/E) Ratio
D/E = Total Debt ÷ Total Shareholders' Equity

D/E reveals how much borrowed money the company relies on vs. its own shareholder capital. A D/E of 2.0 means for every $1 of equity, the company owes $2 to lenders. High debt works fine in boom times but becomes catastrophic when revenue drops — loan payments don't pause for bad quarters. This is the #1 bankruptcy predictor.

📖 Safe vs Danger Zones
▼
D/E RangeSignalWhat It Means
0 – 0.5Very SafeCompany nearly debt-free
0.5 – 1.5HealthyModerate, manageable leverage
1.5 – 3.0WatchLeverage is increasing — monitor
> 3.0DangerHighly vulnerable to downturns
Important: Airlines, utilities, and telecom naturally carry higher D/E by industry norms — always compare against sector peers, not the broad market average.
💵
Fundamental
Free Cash Flow (FCF)
FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures

Accounting profits can be engineered. Cold, hard cash cannot. FCF is the actual real money left over after the company has paid for everything it needs to operate and grow. This cash can fund dividends, stock buybacks, debt repayment, or new acquisitions. Professional investors often call FCF the "king of all financial metrics."

📖 Why FCF is King + What to Look For
▼
🟢 Elite FCF Signals
FCF Margin above 15–20% — elite-quality business
FCF growing consistently faster than revenue
FCF exceeds reported net income — no accounting tricks
🔴 Red Flags
FCF negative for multiple consecutive years in a mature company
FCF shrinking while profits appear to be growing
Apple (AAPL): ~$100B+ FCF/year. Visa (V): 50%+ FCF margins. These are the gold standard.

Note: Negative FCF is acceptable for early-stage companies investing in infrastructure (e.g., Amazon during its first decade of buildout).
💰
Fundamental
Dividend Yield
Yield = Annual Dividend Per Share ÷ Stock Price × 100

Some companies share their profits directly with shareholders as dividends — regular cash payments just for owning the stock. A $100 stock that pays $3/year has a 3% dividend yield. This is essentially the "salary" your investment pays you while you wait for the stock price to grow. Dividend income is the foundation of long-term wealth compounding.

📖 What's a Safe vs Dangerous Yield?
▼
YieldWhat It Signals
0%Growth-focused — reinvesting all profits back in (normal for tech)
1–2%Modest income, still growth-oriented
2–4%Ideal: healthy balance of income + growth + stability
4–6%High yield — verify it's sustainable (check payout ratio)
> 7%Danger zone — often a "dividend trap," may be cut soon
A very high yield (10%+) is often a "dividend trap" — the stock price has collapsed so far that the yield looks attractive, but the company is usually about to cut the dividend entirely.
🏰
Fundamental
Economic Moat
Qualitative: Competitive Advantage Durability Assessment

A moat (like the water around a medieval castle) protects a business from attack by competitors. A company with a strong economic moat can defend its market share, pricing power, and profits for years or decades. Warren Buffett refuses to invest in businesses without wide moats — no matter how cheap they appear.

📖 5 Types of Moats + Real Examples
▼
🏰 The 5 Classic Moat Types
Network Effect: More users = more valuable — nearly impossible to replicate (Visa, Meta, LinkedIn)
Switching Costs: Too painful or expensive for customers to leave (Salesforce, Oracle, SAP)
Cost Advantage: Produces so efficiently that competitors can't match the price (Costco, Amazon, Walmart)
Intangible Assets: Patents, regulatory licenses, or brands so strong they command premium pricing (Apple, Coca-Cola, Pfizer)
Efficient Scale: Market so niche that only one player can profitably serve it (utility companies, toll roads)
"The most important thing to me is figuring out how big a moat there is around the business. What I love, of course, is a big castle and a big moat with piranhas and crocodiles."— Warren Buffett
🌍
Fundamental
Macroeconomic Environment
Context: Interest Rates + Inflation + GDP + Fed Policy

Even the best company can see its stock collapse if the macro tide turns against it. Interest rates, inflation, GDP growth, and Federal Reserve policy all create powerful headwinds or tailwinds that impact every stock in the market. Understanding the macro backdrop is like checking the weather before flying — you can't control it, but ignoring it is reckless.

📖 Key Macro Forces and Their Effects
▼
📉 Rising Interest Rates
Crushes growth stocks (future earnings worth less in today's dollars)
Benefits banks and insurance companies (earn more on loans)
📈 Falling Interest Rates
Rocket fuel for growth stocks, tech, and real estate
Stimulates consumer spending and borrowing across economy
🔥 High Inflation
Good for commodity stocks, energy, gold, and pricing-power businesses
Hurts companies with fixed-price contracts and thin margins
When the Fed raised rates 11× in 2022–2023, the NASDAQ fell 33%. When rates started falling in 2024, tech rallied back to all-time highs within 12 months.

Chapter 2 — The "When to Buy/Sell" Framework
Technical Analysis
Chart-based indicators that tell you the timing — when to actually pull the trigger
🔭
Technical
Trend Identification
Uptrend = Higher Highs + Higher Lows over time

The most fundamental rule in technical analysis: the trend is your friend. If a stock consistently makes higher peaks and higher troughs, it's in an uptrend — a powerful tailwind. If it makes lower peaks and lower troughs, it's in a downtrend — a crushing headwind that destroys buyers every time they try to fight it.

📖 How to Identify Trends + The Biggest Beginner Mistake
▼
🟢 Uptrend Signals
Each rally reaches a higher price than the previous rally peak
Each pullback holds above the previous pullback's lowest point
Stock is trading above both its 50-day and 200-day moving averages
🔴 Downtrend Signals
Each rally fails at a lower price than the last attempt
Each bounce gets sold off aggressively before recovering
The #1 Beginner Mistake: Buying a stock because it's fallen a lot, without checking the trend. A stock in a downtrend can always fall further. Professional traders call this "catching a falling knife."
🛡️
Technical
Support Levels
Historical Price Floor — Buyers Step In Repeatedly

A support level is a price the stock has bounced off multiple times historically. Think of it as a trampoline — every time the stock falls to that price, buyers rush in and propel it back up. The more times a level holds as support, the stronger and more significant it becomes. Support = your best risk-managed entry zone.

📖 How Professional Traders Use Support
▼
📌 The Trading Strategy
Buy near support: Best risk/reward — downside is limited and defined
Set stop-loss just below it: If support breaks, you know you're wrong — exit immediately
Support break: When support fails convincingly, it typically flips to become resistance above
Common support levels: Round numbers ($100, $500), previous swing lows, the 200-day moving average, and Fibonacci retracement levels (38.2%, 61.8%).
🚧
Technical
Resistance Levels
Historical Price Ceiling — Sellers Take Profits Repeatedly

Resistance is the mirror image of support — a price the stock has repeatedly failed to break through. Think of it as a glass ceiling. Every time the stock approaches that level, investors who bought near the top see their chance to break even and flood the market with sell orders, stopping the advance cold and pushing the price back down.

📖 Breakouts — The Most Powerful Trade Setup
▼
🚀 When Resistance Finally Breaks
A breakout above resistance on high volume is one of the most powerful buy signals in technical analysis
Old resistance becomes new support after a confirmed breakout
A breakout on low volume is often a false breakout — highly likely to fail and reverse
All-time highs are the ultimate resistance. When a stock breaks to new all-time highs on strong volume, there are no trapped sellers above — which frequently leads to explosive, sustained moves upward as new demand has no resistance overhead.
📊
Technical
Trading Volume
Volume = Number of Shares Changing Hands in a Session

Volume is the "conviction meter" of the stock market. When a stock makes a big move on high volume, it means institutional investors (hedge funds, pension funds, mutual funds) are participating — and the move is far more likely to hold and extend. A big move on low volume is fragile — it often reverses quickly when nobody else backs it.

📖 The 4 Volume Rules Every Trader Lives By
▼
Price UP + Volume HIGH: Strong institutional accumulation — very bullish
Price UP + Volume LOW: Weak, unconvincing rally — likely to fail soon
Price DOWN + Volume HIGH: Heavy distribution — big money is exiting, very bearish
Price DOWN + Volume LOW: Normal healthy pullback — likely temporary, not alarming
📈
Technical
Moving Averages (50-day & 200-day MA)
MA(N) = Average Closing Price Over Last N Trading Days

Stock prices are incredibly noisy day-to-day. Moving averages smooth out all the random daily jumps and reveal the actual underlying direction. The 50-day MA shows the medium-term trend. The 200-day MA shows the long-term trend. Trillions of dollars of institutional capital automatically buy and sell near these levels.

📖 Golden Cross & Death Cross Explained
▼
⚡ Golden Cross — Major Long-Term Buy Signal
The 50-day MA crosses above the 200-day MA
Signals the beginning of a sustained long-term uptrend
NVDA, AAPL, and META each rallied 40–100%+ following their Golden Cross signals
☠️ Death Cross — Major Long-Term Warning Signal
The 50-day MA crosses below the 200-day MA
Signals the beginning of a sustained long-term downtrend
Many institutional funds are programmed to reduce exposure automatically when this occurs
🌡️
Technical
RSI — Relative Strength Index
RSI Scale: 0 (Max Fear/Oversold) → 100 (Max Greed/Overbought)

RSI is a temperature gauge for market momentum. It measures how fast and how far the stock has moved recently. When RSI climbs above 70, the stock has been bid up too fast — it's "overbought" and likely due for a pullback. When it falls below 30, it's been beaten down excessively — "oversold" and may be a buying opportunity.

📖 RSI Zones, Strategy + Important Caveat
▼
0–30 Buy Zone
Oversold
30–70 Neutral
Neutral
70–100 Sell
Overbought
Important Caveat: During strong bull markets, a stock can stay above RSI 70 for weeks or months. RSI is most powerful when it shows a divergence — the stock makes a new high but RSI does not — which signals weakening momentum even before the price reverses. Never use RSI alone.
🕯️
Technical
Candlestick Patterns
Visual: Open, High, Low, Close per Trading Session

Every trading day creates a "candlestick" on the chart. The body shows where price opened and closed; the wicks (shadows) show the extreme highs and lows. Specific combinations of these candles form patterns that reveal a dramatic shift in market psychology — from fear to greed or greed to fear — often reliably predicting the next price move before it happens.

📖 Key Patterns Every Beginner Needs to Know
▼
🟢 Bullish Reversal Patterns (Buy Signals)
Hammer: Small body, long lower wick — sellers pushed it down hard but buyers took over completely
Bullish Engulfing: A massive green candle completely swallows the prior red — overwhelming buyer conviction
Morning Star: Three-candle reversal pattern — gap down, doji of indecision, then strong green reversal
🔴 Bearish Reversal Patterns (Sell Signals)
Shooting Star: Small body, long upper wick — buyers pushed it up sharply but sellers completely overwhelmed them
Bearish Engulfing: A massive red candle swallows the prior green — sellers brutally overwhelmed buyers
⚡
Technical
MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)
MACD = 12-day EMA − 26-day EMA | Signal = 9-day EMA of MACD

MACD is one of the world's most widely used indicators. In plain English: it compares a shorter-term price average to a longer-term average to detect when short-term momentum is shifting direction. When the MACD line crosses above the signal line, upward momentum is accelerating. When it crosses below, momentum is losing steam — a warning to reconsider your position.

📖 Reading MACD Signals Like a Pro
▼
🟢 Bullish MACD Signals
MACD line crosses above the signal line (bullish crossover) — momentum turning positive
Histogram bars growing taller above zero — acceleration of upward momentum
Both MACD and signal line crossing from below zero to above (strongest buy signal)
🔴 Bearish MACD Signals
MACD line crosses below signal line — momentum turning negative
Divergence: stock makes a new price high but MACD does not match it — momentum is fading
Best practice: Combine MACD with RSI and volume for confirmation. MACD alone produces many false signals in sideways, choppy, or range-bound markets.

Chapter 3 — Protecting Your Capital
Risk Management & Portfolio Fit
The discipline that separates professionals from gamblers — surviving bad streaks and compounding over decades
⚖️
Risk Mgmt
Risk/Reward Ratio
R:R = Potential Profit Target ÷ Maximum Acceptable Loss

Before entering any trade, the professional always calculates: "If I'm right, how much do I make? If I'm wrong, how much do I lose?" The golden rule: only take trades where the potential profit is at least 2–3× the potential loss. With this discipline, even if you only win 40% of your trades, you'll still generate excellent overall returns.

📖 Full Calculation Walkthrough + Standards
▼
Complete Example
Buy NVDA at $900.
Stop-loss at $850 → Risk per share = $50
Price target at $1,050 → Reward per share = $150
R:R Ratio = $150 ÷ $50 = 3:1 ✅ Excellent trade setup
Professional Standards
Below 1:1 — Never trade this. The math is permanently against you
1:1 to 2:1 — Marginal. Only viable with a very high win rate (>60%)
2:1 or better — Acceptable for most active trading strategies
3:1 or better — Ideal. Even 33% win rate is overall profitable
📐
Risk Mgmt
Position Sizing
Shares = (Account × Risk%) ÷ (Entry Price − Stop-Loss Price)

The most underrated skill in all of investing. Position sizing answers the critical question: "How many shares should I buy?" Most professional traders risk only 1–2% of their total account on any single trade. This means 10 consecutive losses only costs 10–20% of the account — not a wipeout. It's the only reason professionals survive the inevitable losing streaks.

📖 The 2% Rule — Step-by-Step Walkthrough
▼
Position Sizing Example
Total Account: $50,000
Max risk per trade (2%): $1,000
Entry price: $200 | Stop-loss: $190
Risk per share: $200 − $190 = $10
Correct position size: $1,000 ÷ $10 = 100 shares max
Risk Tolerance Spectrum
1–2% per trade — Professional standard, survives long losing streaks
3–5% per trade — Aggressive, but survivable with discipline
10%+ per trade — This is gambling, not investing. One bad streak can end you
🧩
Risk Mgmt
Diversification Check
No Single Sector > 25–30% | No Single Stock > 5–10%

Having 10 different tech stocks is not diversification — it's concentration with extra steps. They all fall together when tech falls. True diversification means spreading capital across different sectors (technology, healthcare, financials, energy, consumer staples) so that when any one sector gets crushed, others hold up and protect the portfolio.

📖 Smart Diversification Framework
▼
🏆 Professional Portfolio Rules
No single stock exceeds 5–10% of total portfolio value
No single sector exceeds 25–30% of total portfolio value
Mix growth stocks + value stocks + dividend payers for balance
High-conviction positions: up to 20% is acceptable only after deep, thorough research
"Diversification is protection against ignorance. It makes little sense if you know what you are doing."— Warren Buffett (who concentrates his portfolio heavily in a few high-conviction bets)
⏳
Risk Mgmt
Time Horizon Alignment
Strategy Must Perfectly Match Your Intended Holding Period

A strategy that works brilliantly for a 10-year investor is completely wrong for a 2-week trader — and vice versa. Before entering any position, you must decide exactly how long you intend to hold it. This determines which signals matter, what losses are acceptable to absorb, and what "winning" actually looks like for that specific investment thesis.

📖 Matching Strategy to Holding Period
▼
HorizonStrategyKey Tools
Days–WeeksMomentum TradingRSI, MACD, Volume, Candles
Weeks–MonthsSwing TradingMAs, S/R Levels, Earnings
1–3 YearsGrowth InvestingRevenue Growth, P/E, PEG
5–10+ YearsValue InvestingFCF, ROE, Moat, DCF, Dividends
The Worst Mistake: Buying a stock for a short-term catalyst, watching it fall, and then deciding to hold it indefinitely because you can't accept the loss. Changing your time horizon after the fact to justify avoiding a loss is one of the most destructive habits in investing.

Chapter 4 — The Hardest & Most Important Skill
The Exit Strategy
5 specific triggers that tell you exactly when to sell — pre-defined before you ever buy
⚠️ The Most Common Investor Mistake: Most investors are great at researching what to buy — but have absolutely no plan for when to sell. Buying is the easy, exciting part. Having a pre-defined, emotion-free exit plan before entering a trade is what separates amateurs from professionals. Without one, fear and greed will make every decision for you.
🧠
Trigger 1: Fundamental Breakdown — The Original Thesis is Destroyed
You bought the stock because of a specific thesis — a belief that this business would grow and succeed. If that thesis is no longer valid, you must sell — regardless of whether you're sitting on a profit or a loss. Examples: a massive earnings miss that signals structural decay (not just a bad quarter), the unexpected departure of a visionary CEO, a competitor that makes the product obsolete overnight, a major regulatory action that dismantles the business model, or a scandal that permanently damages the brand.

The test to ask yourself: "If I didn't own this stock today, would I still buy it right now at this current price, knowing exactly what I know?" If the honest answer is no — sell without hesitation.
📊
Trigger 2: Valuation Peak — The Price Has Run Far Beyond Reality
Sometimes the business is still completely healthy and executing perfectly — but the stock price has run so far ahead of reality that the valuation is no longer defensible by any rational measure. When a stock's P/E or PEG ratio reaches bubble-level extremes — far above its historical average and far above what its growth rate can justify — the stock has already "priced in" many years of future growth. Even if execution continues perfectly, the stock can go nowhere for years while earnings slowly "catch up" to the inflated price.

Rule of thumb: When the PEG ratio exceeds 3–4× and the stock is trading at a generational high valuation multiple, taking substantial profits is prudent risk management — even if the company continues to perform.
🛑
Trigger 3: Stop-Loss Hit — Your Pre-Planned Maximum Loss Limit
A stop-loss is a pre-defined price below which you will exit the trade automatically. It's your emotion-free, pre-planned "I was wrong" exit trigger. Professional traders always set their stop-loss before entering a trade — never after. This removes all emotion from the decision. When the stock hits the level: exit. Period. No second-guessing. No "I'll give it another week." No hope-based holding.

Common stop-loss placements: Just below a key support level, below the 50-day or 200-day moving average, or a fixed percentage below entry (7–10% is the most common professional standard). The best traders protect their capital ruthlessly and patiently wait for the next high-quality opportunity — they never fight the tape.
🎯
Trigger 4: Profit Target Reached — Discipline Over Greed
Before entering any trade, you should calculate a specific upside price target based on valuation analysis, technical resistance levels, or analyst consensus. When the stock reaches that target, take your profits — at least partially. Many investors let greed transform their winners into losers by holding too long, watching the inevitable reversal erase months of hard-won gains before finally selling at a loss.

Pro strategy — Scaling Out: Sell 30–50% of your position at your first profit target, then set a trailing stop on the remainder. This locks in realized gains while preserving exposure to potential further upside. You can never go wrong booking profits.
🔄
Trigger 5: Opportunity Cost Pivot — Better Use of Your Capital Emerges
Every dollar sitting in one stock is a dollar that cannot be deployed into a better opportunity. If you identify a stock with 3× more upside potential, superior growth catalysts, and similar or lower risk than your current holding, it may be strategically correct to sell the weaker position and redirect that capital where it can work much harder. This is called an "opportunity cost pivot" and is a hallmark of sophisticated, adaptive portfolio management.

The recurring question to ask: "Is my capital doing the absolute best work it possibly can in this specific stock right now, or is there a significantly superior use of these funds available today?" This should not be a one-time consideration — it should be part of your ongoing portfolio review discipline.
